EB-2: Advanced Degree or Exceptional Ability

The standard EB-2 is the employment-based second preference category for a member of the professions holding an advanced degree, or for a person of exceptional ability in the sciences, arts, or business. This is the most common green card path for skilled professionals whose employer sponsors them. What defines the standard EB-2 is that it is employer-sponsored. Before the immigrant petition is ever filed, the employer has to obtain a PERM labor certification, which is a U.S. Department of Labor process that tests the U.S. labor market, and the employer has to offer the person a permanent job. Only then does the employer file the immigrant petition. This page covers the standard EB-2 with PERM, which is distinct from the EB-2 National Interest Waiver.

EB-2

The EB-2 Test

The standard EB-2 rests on two things at once. First, you have to qualify for the EB-2 classification on one of two eligibility bases. Second, because this is the employer-sponsored version of EB-2, an employer has to run a PERM labor certification and offer you a permanent job, and the employer files the petition. Meeting the eligibility base alone is not enough. Without the PERM labor certification and the job offer, there is no standard EB-2 case.

How EB-2 eligibility works, and why PERM comes first

There are two ways to qualify for the underlying EB-2 classification. The first is the advanced-degree base. The second is the exceptional-ability base. You need one of them, not both.

The advanced-degree base covers a member of the professions who holds an advanced degree or its equivalent. An advanced degree means a U.S. master's degree or higher, or a foreign equivalent degree. It can also be met by a U.S. bachelor's degree, or a foreign equivalent, followed by at least five years of progressive experience in the specialty after the degree. That combination is treated as the equivalent of a master's degree. There is an important second half to this base that people miss. It is not enough that you personally hold an advanced degree. The job the employer is offering, as described in the PERM labor certification, must itself require an advanced degree or the equivalent. If the position only requires a bachelor's degree, the case generally belongs in EB-3, not EB-2, even if you happen to hold a master's.

The exceptional-ability base is for a person who has a degree of expertise significantly above that ordinarily encountered in the sciences, arts, or business. This is a lower standard than the extraordinary-ability standard used for EB-1A, but it is still a meaningful bar. USCIS evaluates it in two steps. First it checks whether you have submitted at least three of six specific types of evidence. Then it makes a final merits determination, weighing all the evidence together to decide whether you really do have expertise significantly above the ordinary. Meeting three criteria on paper is the entry point, not the finish line.

This is the point where the standard EB-2 and the EB-2 National Interest Waiver diverge, so it is worth stating plainly. The EB-2 National Interest Waiver is a subset of EB-2 that waives both the PERM labor certification and the job offer, which lets the person file on their own behalf. The standard EB-2 described on this page does neither. It requires the PERM labor certification, it requires a permanent job offer, and it requires an employer to act as the petitioner. If you do not have an employer willing to sponsor you and run PERM, the standard EB-2 is not open to you, and the National Interest Waiver is the alternative worth reading about.

Now the part that sets the standard EB-2 apart. This path is employer-sponsored, and the PERM labor certification comes first, before the immigrant petition. PERM is a Department of Labor process, run by the employer, that tests the U.S. labor market for the offered position. In broad strokes it has three parts. The employer requests a Prevailing Wage Determination from the Department of Labor to establish the correct wage for the role. The employer then conducts a period of recruitment to test whether there are able, willing, qualified, and available U.S. workers for the position. If that recruitment does not turn up a qualified U.S. worker, the employer files the labor certification application, Form ETA 9089, with the Department of Labor. Only after the Department of Labor certifies the ETA 9089 does the employer file the immigrant petition, Form I-140, with USCIS. Your priority date, which is your place in line, is set on the day the PERM application is filed with the Department of Labor.

The two eligibility bases in plain language

Advanced-degree base. You qualify if you are a member of the professions and you hold one of the following.

  1. A U.S. master's degree or higher, or a foreign degree evaluated to be the equivalent of a U.S. master's degree or higher.
  2. A U.S. bachelor's degree, or a foreign degree evaluated to be the equivalent of a U.S. bachelor's degree, plus at least five years of progressive, post-degree work experience in the specialty. This combination is treated as equivalent to a master's degree.

If the specialty customarily requires a doctorate, you generally must hold a U.S. doctorate or a foreign equivalent. A person who does not hold at least a U.S. bachelor's degree or a foreign equivalent generally cannot use this base. Confirm equivalency with an attorney. Separately, the offered position itself, as certified in the PERM labor certification, must require an advanced degree or the equivalent. Merely holding an advanced degree is not sufficient if the job does not call for one.

Exceptional-ability base. Exceptional ability means a degree of expertise significantly above that ordinarily encountered in the sciences, arts, or business. You must submit at least three of these six types of evidence.

  1. An official academic record showing a degree, diploma, certificate, or similar award from a college, university, school, or other institution of learning relating to your area of exceptional ability.
  2. Letters from current or former employers showing at least ten years of full-time experience in the occupation.
  3. A license to practice the profession, or certification for the particular profession or occupation.
  4. Evidence that you have commanded a salary or other pay for services that demonstrates exceptional ability, relative to others in the field.
  5. Evidence of membership in professional associations.
  6. Evidence of recognition for achievements and significant contributions to the industry or field by peers, governmental entities, or professional or business organizations.

If these standard types of evidence do not readily apply to your occupation, you may be able to submit comparable evidence. Meeting three criteria is only the first step. USCIS then weighs all the evidence together in a final merits determination to decide whether you genuinely have expertise significantly above the ordinary.

The PERM labor certification, step by step

PERM is run by the employer at the U.S. Department of Labor, and it happens before the immigrant petition. The general sequence looks like this.

  1. Prevailing Wage Determination. The employer asks the Department of Labor for a Prevailing Wage Determination to establish the correct wage for the offered role in that location. The employer must be willing and able to pay at least this wage.
  2. Recruitment. The employer conducts a defined period of recruitment for the position to test the U.S. labor market. This includes required job postings and advertising, aimed at finding able, willing, qualified, and available U.S. workers for the role.
  3. File Form ETA 9089. If the recruitment does not identify a qualified U.S. worker, the employer files the labor certification application, Form ETA 9089, with the Department of Labor through its Foreign Labor Application Gateway. The Department of Labor may certify the application, or it may select it for an audit before deciding.
  4. Certification sets the stage for the petition. Once the Department of Labor certifies the ETA 9089, the certified labor certification is what the employer submits with the immigrant petition to USCIS. A certified labor certification is valid for a limited window, so USCIS must receive the I-140 before that window closes.

Your priority date is the date the PERM application is filed with the Department of Labor. That date carries forward and later governs your place in the visa line. For the authoritative rules on PERM, see the U.S. Department of Labor at flag.dol.gov.

EB-2

Self-Assessment

This is a self-assessment to help you think honestly about where you stand. It is not a decision, and it is not a prediction. Only a licensed immigration attorney can evaluate a real case, because eligibility is very fact-specific. Because the standard EB-2 is employer-sponsored, there are really two separate questions to work through. Do you meet one of the two eligibility bases, and do you have an employer willing to sponsor you and run PERM.

Work through where you stand

Start with the eligibility base. On the advanced-degree base, ask whether you hold a U.S. master's degree or higher, or a foreign equivalent. If you hold only a bachelor's degree, ask whether you have at least five years of progressive, post-degree experience in the specialty, and whether you can document that progression. Then ask a separate question that is easy to overlook. Does the job you would be sponsored for actually require an advanced degree or the equivalent. If the role only calls for a bachelor's degree, the case likely belongs in EB-3, even if your own credentials are higher.

If the advanced-degree base is not a clean fit, consider the exceptional-ability base. Walk through the six types of evidence in the test above and ask, for each one, whether you have concrete and documentable proof. You need at least three. Be honest about quality, because meeting three criteria is only the entry point, and USCIS makes a final judgment on the whole picture.

Now the sponsorship question, which is what makes this the standard EB-2 rather than the National Interest Waiver. Do you have a U.S. employer who is willing to offer you a permanent, full-time position and to run the PERM labor certification for that role. PERM takes time and costs the employer money, so a genuine commitment matters. If the answer is no, the standard EB-2 is not available to you as described here.

That last point is the honest fork in the road. If you meet an EB-2 eligibility base but do not have a sponsoring employer, or you would rather not depend on one, the EB-2 National Interest Waiver is the alternative to read about. It waives both PERM and the job offer and lets you file on your own behalf, but you carry the case yourself and you have to make a higher, national-interest argument. A licensed immigration attorney can help you weigh the standard employer-sponsored EB-2 against the National Interest Waiver, and against EB-3, for your specific facts.

EB-2

PERM Versus NIW

If there is one insight worth carrying away, it is this. The standard EB-2 and the EB-2 National Interest Waiver land you in the exact same visa line, but they get you there in very different ways, and the trade-off between them is about control and effort, not about a faster queue.

How the trade-off actually works

The standard EB-2 is employer-dependent. Your employer runs the PERM labor certification and files the Form I-140 petition. That dependency has real downsides. Your case is tied to that employer and that job, and PERM adds many months before the petition can even be filed. It also has real upsides. The employer usually pays for the required PERM costs, the legal argument is comparatively straightforward when the employer is genuinely committed, and you are not the one who has to build and defend the case.

The National Interest Waiver flips that. You self-petition, so the case is portable and not tied to one employer, and you skip PERM entirely. In exchange, you carry the case yourself, and you have to make a higher argument that waiving the job offer and labor certification is in the national interest. That is a heavier lift and a different standard of proof.

Here is the part that surprises people. Whichever route you take, you end up in the same EB-2 queue. After the petition is approved, most people still wait for an immigrant visa number to become available, and that wait depends on your category and your country of birth. It is governed by the monthly Visa Bulletin. The National Interest Waiver does not put you in a faster line than the standard EB-2. It removes the PERM and job-offer requirements, but it does not change the queue. One meaningful difference in timing is upstream of the queue, not in it. Because PERM can add many months to well over a year before the petition is filed, the standard EB-2 can take longer to reach an approved petition, while the National Interest Waiver skips that step. The line itself, though, is the same EB-2 line either way.

None of this is a prediction. Visa Bulletin movement changes month to month and can move backward as well as forward. Before you rely on any timing assumption, check the current Visa Bulletin from the U.S. Department of State and confirm where your category and country of birth actually stand. You can also see the History and Trends charts for how the lines have moved over time.

EB-2

Process and Timeline

The standard EB-2 process has a distinct shape because it is employer-sponsored, and the PERM labor certification comes first. Unlike a self-petition, you do not drive the opening stage. Your employer runs PERM at the Department of Labor and then files the immigrant petition. Here are the stages in order.

Step 1: The employer completes the PERM labor certification (Department of Labor)

Before any petition is filed, the employer requests a Prevailing Wage Determination from the Department of Labor, conducts the required recruitment to test the U.S. labor market, and then files Form ETA 9089 with the Department of Labor. If the Department of Labor certifies it, the employer has a certified labor certification to use in the next step. Your priority date is set on the date the PERM application is filed. The Department of Labor may also select the application for an audit, which adds time. See the Department of Labor at flag.dol.gov.

Step 2: The employer files the I-140 petition (USCIS)

With the certified labor certification in hand, the employer files Form I-140, the Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers, with USCIS. In the standard EB-2, the employer is the petitioner, not you. The petition must be supported by the certified labor certification, and the employer must show its ability to pay the offered wage from the priority date onward.

Step 3: Consider premium processing (optional)

The employer can add Form I-907 to request premium processing, which asks USCIS to act on the I-140 within a set number of business days. This speeds up the decision on the petition. It does not move you up the visa line.

Step 4: Wait for your priority date, if needed

Your place in line is set by your priority date, which is the PERM filing date, and it is governed by the monthly Visa Bulletin. Depending on your category and country of birth, a visa number may be available right away or you may wait for years. Premium processing speeds up only the I-140 decision. It has no effect on this priority-date wait.

Step 5: Adjust status or go through consular processing

If you are in the United States and a visa number is available to you, you can file Form I-485 to adjust status to permanent resident. If you are outside the United States, you go through consular processing at a U.S. consulate abroad. Which path applies depends on where you are and on visa availability.

EB-2

Requests for Evidence

First, take a breath. A Request for Evidence, or RFE, is not a denial. It is USCIS telling you that, based on what has been submitted so far, it is not yet convinced, and it is giving the petitioner a chance to send more. Many approved EB-2 cases went through an RFE first.

What EB-2 RFEs focus on, and how to respond

Standard EB-2 RFEs tend to cluster around a few themes. On the advanced-degree base, a common theme is proving the degree itself, or proving the equivalency when the case rests on a bachelor's degree plus five years of progressive experience. USCIS may ask for a credential evaluation of a foreign degree, or for detailed employment letters that show the five years of experience were both post-degree and genuinely progressive in the specialty. On the exceptional-ability base, RFEs often question whether the evidence really satisfies at least three of the six criteria, and whether the whole record shows expertise significantly above the ordinary at the final merits step.

Another cluster is specific to the employer-sponsored structure. USCIS may raise job-requirement questions, asking whether the offered position, as described in the labor certification, actually requires an advanced degree or the equivalent, since that is what places the case in EB-2 rather than EB-3. USCIS may also raise ability-to-pay questions on the I-140, asking the employer to show it can pay the offered wage from the priority date until you become a permanent resident, through tax returns, annual reports, or audited financial statements.

One thing to keep separate in your mind. PERM audits happen earlier and at a different agency. An audit is a Department of Labor step during the labor certification, not a USCIS Request for Evidence on the I-140. They are different events at different stages.

For any RFE, the deadline printed on the actual notice is the one that governs, so read the notice carefully and calendar that date. The notice also tells you specifically what USCIS wants, so the response should answer those specific points directly. Drafting an RFE response is a job for a licensed immigration attorney, and in an employer-sponsored case the employer's counsel usually prepares it.

EB-2

Working With Counsel

The standard EB-2 is employer-sponsored, and that shapes how legal help works. In most cases the employer's law firm runs the PERM labor certification and files the Form I-140, because the employer is the petitioner and the party required to run PERM. This is a short literacy guide for understanding that relationship. It does not endorse, rank, or recommend any specific firm, and it does not describe any employer's internal immigration policy.

The most important thing to understand is who the employer's counsel represents. That firm represents the employer, not you, even though the case is about your green card and you are the beneficiary. In practice the two interests usually line up, because the employer wants the case approved too. But they are not identical, and it helps to know that going in.

What that means for you as the employee
  • You are the beneficiary of the petition, but the employer is the petitioner and the employer's counsel represents the employer.
  • The employer generally controls key decisions, such as whether and when to run PERM, how the job is described, and whether to add premium processing.
  • Your priority date, set at PERM filing, is valuable. Understand what happens to it if you change jobs, since that is a fact-specific question worth asking.
What to ask the employer's counsel
  • What is the expected timeline for each stage, from the Prevailing Wage Determination through PERM, the I-140, and adjustment of status.
  • How is the offered position being described, and does it require an advanced degree or the equivalent, which is what keeps the case in EB-2.
  • Who handles an RFE if one comes, and how will you be kept informed.
  • What copies of filings and receipts will you receive for your own records.
Your own documents and your own attorney
  • Ask for copies of what is filed on your behalf, including the certified labor certification, the I-140 receipt and approval, and any RFE and response. These are important to your own record.
  • You can request your own immigration records from USCIS through a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request.
  • You are free to consult your own licensed immigration attorney for personal advice, separate from the employer's counsel, especially on questions like job changes, timing, and whether the National Interest Waiver is worth pursuing in parallel.
